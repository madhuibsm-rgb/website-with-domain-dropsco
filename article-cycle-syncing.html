A NAME CHANGE, A METABOLIC REFRAME

Eating for Your Cycle:
What the Evidence Actually Says

Cycle syncing isn’t a trend — it’s biology. A registered dietitian separates the facts, the food, and the myths.

12 min read · The Explorer’s Table — Issue 01 · Hormones & Metabolism
Reading now
0% read

They changed the name. The part worth noticing is why. In May, polycystic ovary syndrome stopped being called polycystic ovary syndrome. After years of patient advocacy, a global consensus renamed it polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome — PMOS — because the old label was misleading, stigmatizing and clinically incomplete.

The old name pointed a torch at the ovaries. The new one points toward what matters more: hormone disruption, insulin resistance and metabolic risk. That shift is not cosmetic. It changes what gets measured, what gets diagnosed and what gets treated first.

What was actually going on underneath

“Polyendocrine” acknowledges multiple interacting hormonal disturbances — insulin, androgens and neuroendocrine hormones — rather than an isolated ovarian problem. “Metabolic” names the insulin resistance that is present in many women with the condition even when BMI looks normal, and that helps explain the increased long-term risk of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease and fatty liver.

The insulin numbers are the ones to sit with. Insulin resistance drives extra androgen production, which disrupts follicle development and stops ovulation. The irregular cycle is the last domino, not the first.

The Indian numbers are their own argument

We finally have proper national data, and it is recent. The ICMR task force study screened 9,824 women aged 18 to 40 across India. Weighted national prevalence came in at 7.2% by the stricter NIH criteria and 19.6% by Rotterdam criteria. Roughly one in five, by the diagnostic standard most Indian clinicians actually use.

The comorbidity picture is the part that should have made front pages. Among Indian women with the condition: 91.9% had dyslipidaemia, 32.9% had non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, 24.9% had metabolic syndrome, 43.2% had obesity and 8.3% had hypertension. Nine in ten with disordered lipids. A third with fatty liver.

And then the thing that makes this specifically ours. South Asian bodies carry more intra-abdominal and truncal fat than white European bodies at the same weight, plus more fat deposited in the liver and skeletal muscle — which produces greater insulin resistance at a lower BMI. That is why Asian Indian cut-offs are lower and why a woman can be told her BMI is “fine” while her risk is already rising.

What actually moves the needle

The 2023 international guideline puts lifestyle first — not because it is the gentle option, but because it targets the mechanism. On the plate, there is no single magic diet. What consistently works is lowering the glycaemic load. In an Indian kitchen that often means restructuring the meal rather than eliminating foods: less refined starch, more protein, and less reliance on rice-heavy plates that turn into blood-sugar events.

Protein is the gap most urban Indian women have. Most of us are running well under what is needed to protect muscle, and muscle is where glucose goes. Curd, paneer, eggs, chicken, fish, dals paired with grains, and whey if that is easier — spread across the day rather than dumped into dinner.

The other lever nobody mentions: sleep and meal timing. Eating dinner at 10:30 pm because that is when the day ends is a metabolic decision, not a scheduling one.

On movement, the baseline is 150 minutes a week of moderate activity or 75 minutes of vigorous activity. For weight change and prevention of regain, the target rises to 250 minutes moderate or 150 minutes vigorous, plus strength training on two non-consecutive days. And because insulin action is the point, the guidance recommends exercising daily, or at minimum every second day.

That reframes everything. Insulin sensitivity improves after a session and fades within roughly 48 hours. Three long weekend workouts and five sedentary days is not the same stimulus as thirty minutes most days.

On supplements, inositol is the one every algorithm will serve you. The evidence is promising but not definitive, and the 2023 guideline points to shared decision-making rather than blanket use. Vitamin D is worth correcting if you are deficient, which in this country, with this much indoor life, you probably are. Test rather than guess.

The one thing to take away

If you were told at twenty-four that you had PCOS, handed a strip of pills and told to come back when you wanted a baby, you were given a reproductive diagnosis for a metabolic condition. That was the state of the science then. It is not now.

The questions worth asking at your next appointment are not about your ovaries. They are fasting insulin, HbA1c, a full lipid panel, a liver ultrasound if there is any flag and a waist measurement against the 80 cm line rather than the BMI chart on the wall.

The new name exists to make that conversation the default one.
